Farro!
-
Ranked!
-
3.1 grams of fat. Saturated fat is only 0.5 grams, a strong mark that keeps the fat profile light. Omega 3 is 100 milligrams, far too little to make farro a useful omega 3 source. Fat is not a grain's main job, but this section is clean rather than powerful.
-
72.1 grams of carbs. Starch is 61.7 grams, providing the grain's main source of energy. Sugar is 0 grams, but the glycemic index is still 63, which is only moderate. This is a good lesson: low sugar does not automatically mean low glycemic, because starch also becomes glucose. For grains, carb behaviour matters more than carb amount.
-
12.6 grams of protein. Essential amino acids score 8 out of 9, so most of the amino acid groups your body must get from food clear the useful threshold. Nonessential amino acids score 8 out of 11, but these matter less because the body can make them. Bioavailability is 80%, a solid result for grain protein. For grains, this is valuable extra support, although carbs remain the main role.
-
Vitamins are low across the board. Vitamin E is the best mark at only 5% daily value. Vitamin E helps protect cells from oxidative damage, but this amount is too small to make farro a meaningful source. For grains, that leaves the energy, protein, and mineral sections doing the work.
-
Zinc is 36% daily value, a strong amount for immune function and tissue repair. Calcium is only 2% daily value, so farro contributes very little towards bone mineral needs. Minerals can make a grain more than just starch, and zinc manages that here.
-
The upsides first: chewy texture holds up in full meals. It stays distinct in soups, salads, and grain bowls instead of turning to mush. Batch-cooks into a practical meal base. One pot can cover several meals and carry stronger toppings. Pearled farro is easier to cook. Removing some outer bran shortens the cooking time and makes the grain more approachable.
-
The drawbacks next: pearling strips away part of the bran. Bran is the fibre and micronutrient-rich outer layer, so easier cooking comes with a nutrition tradeoff. Contains gluten. Because farro is wheat, it is unsuitable for coeliac disease and gluten-free diets. Still needs cooking and planning. It is less instant than rice or couscous and definitely is not grab-and-go.
-
Best bits are strong grain protein, a clean fat profile, useful zinc, and a practical chewy meal role. Weak spots are the moderate glycemic index, very low vitamins and calcium, plus some nutrition lost through pearling. Best for energy and practical meals: the starch fuels activity, while the protein and sturdy texture make it a useful base for bowls, soups, and salads. This is a solid grain, but it still needs other foods around it for a rounded meal.
-
B tier.
