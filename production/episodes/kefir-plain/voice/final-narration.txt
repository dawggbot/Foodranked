Plain Kefir!
-
Ranked!
-
1 gram of fat. Saturated fat is only 0.66 grams and cholesterol is just 5 milligrams, so there is not much fat baggage here. Polyunsaturated fat is only 0.05 grams though, meaning kefir is not bringing much useful unsaturated fat either. For dairy, keeping the saturated fat and cholesterol down is a useful win.
-
4.8 grams of carbs. Most of that comes from lactose, the natural sugar found in milk. The glycemic index is around 30, so it should behave fairly steadily compared with sugary drinks. Fibre is 0 grams, which is completely normal for dairy but means your fruit, grains, or vegetables still need to handle digestion support. For dairy, carbs are mostly a sugar check.
-
3.8 grams of protein. That gives the drink some substance, but it is not enough to make kefir a serious protein food on its own. It can help at breakfast or in a smoothie, but something like Greek yogurt brings much more protein in the same amount. For dairy, protein matters, and kefir is more of a supporter than main character.
-
Vitamin A is 19% daily value, a useful amount for vision, skin, and immune support. Vitamin B12 is 12% daily value, helping nerves and red blood-cell production. Vitamin D is only 5% daily value, so it adds a little bone and immune support but is not doing much heavy lifting. For dairy, this is a decent little vitamin mix rather than a standout one.
-
Calcium is 10% daily value, giving some useful bone and tooth support. Magnesium and potassium are both around 3% daily value, so they barely help with muscle function, fluid balance, or the wider mineral picture. For dairy, calcium is the main mineral job, and kefir does that reasonably well, but without offering much else.
-
The upsides first: traditional kefir grains are tiny microbe communities. They are reusable clusters of bacteria and yeast, not cereal grains, which is honestly a pretty strange little food fact. Fermentation can make lactose easier to handle. A small human study found plain kefir was better tolerated than milk by adults who struggle to digest lactose. The drinkable format is genuinely handy. Have it straight from the fridge, blend it into a smoothie, or use it as a tangy base for dressings.
-
The drawbacks next: sweetened bottles can change the whole deal. Added sugar can turn a useful fermented drink into something much closer to a liquid dessert. Milk allergy is still a hard stop. Fermentation can change the lactose, but it does not remove the milk proteins that trigger an allergy. The sour, slightly funky flavour is not for everyone. Plain kefir tastes much tangier than ordinary milk or sweetened yogurt.
-
Best bits are the light fat profile, useful vitamin A, some calcium, and the genuinely interesting fermentation story. Weak spots are modest protein, no fibre, low magnesium and potassium, and how quickly sweetened versions can lose the plot. Best for an easy fermented breakfast drink, smoothies, or adding some dairy variety without loads of saturated fat. Keep it plain and let fruit handle the sweetness.
-
C tier.
